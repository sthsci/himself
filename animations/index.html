Science animations

These four silent animations follow a predator–prey cycle, show why gold looks bright in one electron microscope and dark in another, and trace how a flow cytometer turns passing cells into dots on a plot. Each film runs for 27 seconds.

Mathematical models

The Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model

Each prey birth, successful hunt or predator death moves the point on the population plot. Its jagged path follows the simulated events. The smooth curve shows the prediction from the Lotka–Volterra equations. Predator numbers keep rising after prey numbers peak. As prey become scarce, predators decline and the prey population recovers.

Materials science techniques

SEM: why gold looks bright

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) sweeps its beam across gold nanoparticles on carbon. Here the detector counts electrons scattered back from the sample. More return from gold than from carbon, so the particles appear bright.

TEM: why gold looks dark

In bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electrons pass through a thin carbon film dotted with gold particles. Gold scatters more electrons beyond the objective aperture than carbon does. Fewer reach the camera from those spots, so the particles appear dark.

Biological techniques

Flow cytometry: one cell, one dot

White blood cells pass through a laser one at a time. Each dot records how much light one cell scatters forward and to the side. In this simulation, monocytes and granulocytes overlap in forward scatter, but side scatter helps distinguish them.
